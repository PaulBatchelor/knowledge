4.1 is extrema: maxima and minima. you can an interval, a closed interval, and there's going to be a largest and smallest value which are maxima and minima, or extrema. specifically, absolute extrema.
---
how do you find absolute extrema? functions have hills and valleys. and at those peaks are were potential extrema lie. also, the endpoints could be the location of absolute extrema. the hills and valleys are location of a certain kind of extrema called local extrema or relative extrema. it turns out that the slopes there are horizontal and flat, which means the derivative is 0. The points where the derivative is 0 are known as critical points, and they are candidates for local extrema. so, the algorithm for finding absolute extrema involves getting the derivative function, finding the critical points by solving for 0, plugging in those points and the endpoints into the function, and determining the min/max.
---
but what if you wanted to determine if a critical point was a local min or max? if you climb a hill, there is no more uphill, and downhill before you; the slope is positive and then negative around you. if a nearby points go from postive to negative around a critical point, that's a local maximum. likewise, a local minimum goes from postive to negative. this loosely describes the first derivative test. you take a function on an interval, find the critical points, and use that to split up the interval. check the sign of each interval, and changes in sign indicate a local extrema.
---
hills and valleys indicate concepts of concavity. the second derivative can be used to detect this. an interval that is a hill is concave down and will have a negative seond derivative. concave up is a valley. thus, you can determine if something is a local minima or maxima by plugging in a critcal point into a the second derivative and checking the sign. this is the second derivative test.
---
concavity goes on a bit more. similar to finding critical points, you can find zeros on the second derivative and break those up into intervals, positive intervals are concave up, negative are down. if there's a sign change between the intervals, we call that zero point an inflection point which changes concavity.
---
pitfalls with extrema: forgetting the endpoints, 0 is ambiguous, knowing when the problem is asking for concavity intervals vs change in sign. there's just a lot of bookkeeping with these, so be organized.

The curriculum, as it stands, was broken up into 3 major components: limits, differentiation, integration. Limits were used to describe differentiation. Differentiation was used to describe integration. The fundamental theorem of calculus was used to tie it all together.
---
It was decided that limits were a suitable introduction to calculus, as a ramp up into derivatives. This seems to be the common path, though Apostol teaches integration first. Do they teach limits to integration there? We learned the way limits can be used to analyze the behavior of functions. Limits are used a tool to precisely define the idea of continuity, an important characteristic in calculus. The rules of continuity are what allow direct substitution to work for limits. Limits and "limit notation" are also used to define the derivative: you have some kind of quotient, which compares the difference between two points of a function and the difference between their values. Limits help us say as this difference between points approach zero, this relationship converges on a value we call the derivative.
---
Excercises in limits often surround the idea of evaluating a limit and getting some concrete value. This mostly obeys the laws of highschool algebra. There are many small rules and tricks that can be used. Most equations we come across can be evaluated with direct substitution because of rules proven to be true. Sometimes a quotient returns 0 over 0, which is a special form which is an indeterminant results. Factoring can sometimes cancel out the zero, but there is also L'Hopitals rule. Absolute value is a tricky one for me because you can't directly take the limit of it using direct substitution; the absolute value is actually one of two functions that need to be looked at. Oftentimes a limit will go to a value where the absolute value is 0, at which point, you'd need to look at both sides of the limit and see if the value adds up.
---
Asymptotes are another thing expressed with limits. Vertical and horizontal. Like left and right, these are hard for my brain to get right. One sided limits are called left and right sided limits, so maybe this is why limits are so challenging to me? Asymptote problems are almost always quotients because those require you to do more stuff usually.
---
Derivatives. I don't have much to say on taking derivatives. For this class, we learn a handful of theorems and taking the derivative turns into a mechanical process. Sum rule, product rule, quotient rule starts things off. Polynomials and simple compound expressions using arithmetic can be evaluated this way.  Expressions that are made up of composed simple functions can be evaluated using the chain rule. When that all feels comfortable, they introduce implicit differentiation, which is taking the derivative plus some algebra. With that, they can introduce applied problems, aka related rates.
---
Related rates are an application of implicit differentation. These problems are usually based on simple geometry. Because it's calculus, this geometry needs to change over time. Geometry can be expressed using an equation, and this equation shows a relationship between two aspects of the geometry. For example, the relationship between the area of a circle and its radius can be expressed as $A=\pi r^2$. When you differentiate this expression, it usually means these variables can change over time. These relationships change at different rates, hence the name related rates.
---
The recipe for solving a related rates problem typically has involved identifying the geometry in question, using implicit differentiation on it, re-arranging the equation with alegebra to solve for a particular value, then plugging in supplied values using direct subsitution or algebra to solve.
---
Derivatives can be used to find extrema in a given function, and we spent some time on this. Local extrema, global extrema. Critical points are points where the derivative is zero. Critical points segment function into intervals, and these intervals can be used to check where a function is increasing and decreasing. Positive to negative change around a point is a local max, and vice versa is a local min. Visualizing a parabola helps here. Checking the sign of value of the critical point at the second derivative is another way to do it: negative is concave down, so local max, positive is concave up, so local min. Visualize the parabola for this.

Concavity. Informally, it describes the way a function curves. It either goes concave up or down. The sign of the first derivative tells if a function is increasing or decreasing, the sign of the second derivative tells if it is concave up or down (useful for extrema). A function can be any combination of increasing/decreasing and concave up/down. To build up intuition for this: take a parabola and split it down the middle. Draw two lines towards the middle, one slopes down, one slopes up. For something like $x^2$, both sides are concave up, but one decays quickly and slows down (negative slope, concave up), and the other increases slowly at but gets faster (positive slope, concave up). Repeat for $-x^2$.
---
Extrema can be used for optimization problems, which spiritually feel like related rates problems. Both are word problems where you need to describe the problem using equations, and fill in some blanks, use a calculus tool with some algebra to solve. An optimization problem has two parts: a optimization equation, and constraints. You start with an equation describing what you want to optimize, and then usually there's an equation describing the constraints. Usually some algebra with the constraint function can allow you to define the optimization function in terms of a single variable. At that point, it becomes an extrema problem.
---
Anti-derivatives are a precursor to integrals. Similar to derivative calculations, it's a pretty mechanical process. Lots of substitution.
---
Anti-derivatives are used to compute indefinite integrals. More or less equivalent, it's mostly just different notation. That notation change yields the canonical expression of definite integrals. Riemann sums are in there too. It's all a little bit jumbled.

The way it was presented, we learned the fundamental theorem of calculus was basically presented backwards so we could compute definite integrals. They just tell you that the diffrerence of two anti-derivatives at the endpoints gives you the integral, and that can be used right away since we knew the rest of it.

Usually this just "works out", but sometimes you need to bring out a constant using the constant multiple rule.
